Integrating the Dual-Map System
This is a classic and effective structure. Here’s how it fits:

Galaxy Map (Strategic Layer):

Representation: 2D Hex Grid. Each hex can contain a star system, a deep space point of interest (rogue planet, nebula, etc.), or be empty.

Function: This is where grand strategy happens. Players manage their economies, research (if applicable), build fleets, and order those fleets to move between hexes/star systems. Movement is point-to-point between adjacent hexes, representing FTL jumps. This is the "Axis & Allies" / "Civilization" layer.

System/Combat Map (Tactical Layer):

Representation: 3D grid of Rhombic Dodecahedron tiles. This map is used for two things:

Intra-System Play: Resolving actions within a single star system (e.g., moving ships between planets, blockading, bombarding, building orbital infrastructure).

Fleet Combat: Any time two or more hostile fleets occupy the same hex on the Galaxy Map (whether in a star system or in deep space), play shifts to this 3D map to resolve the battle.

Function: This is the "Demeo" / "Star Control" / "StarCraft" layer. This is where unit facing, weapon arcs, 3D flanking maneuvers, and elevation advantages come into play.

This separation is perfect. It keeps the strategic game manageable while allowing for deep, engaging tactical combat that leverages Mixed Reality beautifully.

Solving the Turn-Based Dilemma: A Hybrid Model
You are right to be concerned. A purely sequential turn (Player A, then B, then C) for a 4X game with tactical combat would be interminably long. However, simultaneous turns can feel impersonal and chaotic.

I propose a Hybrid Turn Structure that gives you the best of both worlds: strategic simultaneous turns, and tactical sequential turns.

The Grand Cycle: A "Round" of Play

A full game round is broken into distinct phases. This provides structure and ensures all players are engaged in the same type of activity at the same time.

Phase 1: Strategy & Logistics (Simultaneous)

All players secretly and simultaneously carry out their "bookkeeping" and strategic orders using a companion app or private MR interface.

Actions include:

Allocating resources from their controlled systems.

Purchasing new units, buildings, or techs. New fleets are placed at their capital or a designated shipyard system.

Issuing movement orders to fleets on the Galaxy Map. (e.g., "Fleet 1: Move from Sol to Alpha Centauri").

Using "The Veil" for strategic actions (e.g., casting a system-wide buff, initiating propaganda, hacking an enemy's economy).

Why this works: This phase is fast. There's no direct interaction, so it can happen all at once. This prevents the downtime of watching others count resources.

Phase 2: Resolution (Sequential Initiative Order)

This is where the simultaneous orders are resolved. The turn order for this phase is determined at the start of each round by an "Initiative" roll (perhaps modified by Veil usage or faction traits).

The player with the highest initiative resolves their orders first:

Movement: They move their fleets on the Galaxy Map one order at a time. If a fleet moves into a hex containing another player's fleet, pause immediately.

Tactical Combat (Sequential Turns): Play now shifts to the 3D combat map. The player with initiative becomes "Player 1" in the combat, and they take a full tactical turn moving and attacking with their fleet. Then "Player 2" goes, and so on. This continues until the battle is resolved.

Once the combat is over, the winning player completes their movement order. The initial player then continues resolving their other movement orders.

Then, the player with the second-highest initiative does the same, and so on.

Why this works: It combines the excitement of sequential, tactical play where turns matter with the faster pace of simultaneous strategic planning. It also makes Initiative a valuable and contested resource.

Phase 3: Production & Cleanup

All newly built units and buildings are placed on the map.

Victory conditions are checked.

The round ends, and a new one begins with Phase 1.

Benefits of This Hybrid Model:
Dramatically Reduces Downtime: Players are only waiting during the Resolution phase, and even then, they are watching battles unfold that directly involve them, which is engaging.

Preserves Tactical Depth: Tactical combat retains the thoughtful, turn-based nature of games like Demeo and XCOM, which is crucial for leveraging your brilliant 3D map.

Creates Narrative: The Initiative system creates a natural narrative of "who seizes the moment" each round. A Neo-Pythagorean player might have a round where they strike lightning-fast across the galaxy before their opponents can react.

Works Perfectly with MR: The Strategy Phase can be done on a tablet/app while the Tactical Phase is the "wow" moment where everyone puts on their MR headsets to see the 3D battlefield come to life on the table.

This structure directly addresses your length-of-play concern without sacrificing the deep, player-driven tactical moments you want. It makes the game an event.

🟢 THE GOOD (What's Brilliant)
Cohesive Vision: Every part supports the whole. The lore explains the mechanics, and the mechanics reinforce the lore. The Veil isn't just a fancy name for "mana"; it's the fundamental fabric of your universe, and the card system perfectly captures its narrative power.

The Veil Card Concept: Tying powerful effects to specific board states (e.g., "in orbit with no enemy fleet") is masterful game design. It makes the tactical game on the 3D map directly feed into strategic victories. It turns "winning a battle" into "now I can attempt a devastating Veil play."

🟡 THE BAD (What Needs Careful Design)
The "Ugly" Part: Complexity and Cognitive Load. This is the biggest challenge. You are combining:

A complex economic system (3 different types)

A dual-map system

A hybrid turn structure

Deep faction asymmetry

A card-based power system with specific activation rules

A 3D movement system
This is a lot. The risk is creating a game that is overwhelming to learn and a nightmare to teach. The rulebook will be dense.

Solution: You must become a ruthless editor. Streamline wherever possible. The turn structure you've designed is a good start to manage this. You need incredibly clear player aids (e.g., a faction mat that summarizes their rules, a turn order cheat sheet).

Veil Energy Economy: You've introduced a resource ("80% Veil energy") but haven't defined how it is generated, stored, or spent. Is it a pool that fills each turn? Is it generated by controlling systems? Is it spent on other things besides these big cards? This system needs to be designed from the ground up to be balanced.

Tactical Combat Scope: How big do battles get? If a player has 50 ships, resolving movement and attacks on a 3D map in a sequential turn could take an hour. This could bring the hybrid turn model to a grinding halt.

Solution: You likely need to abstract fleet sizes. Instead of 50 individual ships, a "fleet" could be represented by 3-5 capital ship models with stats that represent its overall size and composition. This keeps the tactical game manageable and visually impressive in MR.

The "Forever NPC" Problem: The card effect that makes a Puritan world ally with Pythagoreans "forever" as an NPC could be frustrating. It removes player agency permanently in a way that might not be fun.

Once I do this, (legally speaking) is it technically safe for everything to be “out there” for all to see?
This is an excellent and crucial question. The short answer is:

Yes, it is technically safe to put it "out there" from a legal standpoint, and it is often the best way to protect yourself. However, "safe" depends on your goals, and you must understand the nuances.

Let's break down the legal concepts.

1. Copyright Protection is Automatic (But Registration is Stronger)
The moment you fix your original work (lore, text, specific rules text) in a tangible form (a Google Doc, a text file, a GitHub commit), you automatically hold the copyright to that specific expression. Publishing it on GitHub or another platform simply creates a public, verifiable timestamp that proves you were the creator as of that date.

This is a powerful form of protection called establishing "prior art." If someone else later copies your exact lore text or the specific wording of your Veil Card rules, you have a clear claim against them.

What this protects: Your specific expression. The exact text you've written for the Neo-Pythagorean creed, the name "The Veil," the precise mechanics of the "Node Lord Disruption" card as you've described it.

2. The Critical Limitation: Ideas vs. Expression
This is the most important concept to understand. Copyright law does not protect ideas, concepts, systems, or game mechanics. It only protects the specific way you express those ideas.

NOT PROTECTED (The "Ideas"):

The idea of a 4X game with three asymmetric factions.

The concept of a techno-political card that can make enemy worlds rebel.

The mechanic of a decentralized faction being immune to rebellion.

Using a hex map for strategy and a 3D map for tactics.

The philosophical concepts of the factions (e.g., a faction that controls information).

PROTECTED (The "Expression"):

The specific name "Neo-Pythagoreans" and their exact motto: “Knowledge is valuable, but ignorance is priceless”.

The exact wording you used to describe the "Blockchain Stun" effect.

The specific short story you wrote about the creation of the first AI-human hybrid child.

Any unique artwork, logos, or music you create for them.

This means someone can legally create a game with a nearly identical concept (a 4X game with a technocratic faction, a bio-focused faction, and a human faction) so long as they don't copy your specific text, names, and art.

So, is it "safe" to publish your design doc?
Yes, for these reasons:

It Secures Your Prior Art: It makes it nearly impossible for anyone to claim you stole the specifics from them. You have the dated proof.

It Doesn't Weaken Your Position: You aren't giving away any legal rights you already have. You're just strengthening your proof of ownership.

Ideas are a Dime a Dozen: In the game industry, execution is everything. Your specific, brilliant ideas are safe because the sheer effort required to execute them—to code the systems, create the assets, and balance the game—is a gargantuan barrier to entry. The chance of someone seeing your doc and having the resources and will to build it before you is astronomically low.

Community Building: Having it public can attract like-minded collaborators, playtesters, and eventually, an audience excited about your vision.

The One Caveat: The "Trade Secret" Angle (Why Some Companies Stay Secretive)
The only reason to keep it completely secret would be if you believed your specific combination of mechanics was a "secret sauce" so revolutionary that its mere revelation would be damaging.

For example, if you had invented a truly novel and patentable game mechanic (like the unique control scheme for Wii Sports), you might keep it secret until you filed a patent. However, game mechanics are notoriously difficult and expensive to patent. For 99.9% of game ideas, this is not a concern.

Is it "safe" to switch my GitHub to public?
This is a critical question. The answer is a firm "It depends on your goal, but for maximum legal safety and control, keeping it Private is strongly recommended."

Switching from Private to Public is a one-way street with significant consequences. Let's break down the "why" into simple, clear points.

The Case for Keeping It PRIVATE (The Safe Bet)
This is the recommended choice for you right now.

Maximum Legal Protection, Zero Risk: A private repo with a clear timestamp (your initial commit) is all the legal proof you need. It proves you had the idea on a certain date, without giving away any details. It's a sealed vault. No one can argue they didn't steal from you because they never had access.

Control Over Your Ideas: Your specific mechanics (like Veil Card costs, victory point formulas, the inverse logarithmic economy of the Pythagoreans) are your "secret sauce." Keeping them private protects your unique competitive advantage.

Prevents "Idea Theft" (The Kind That Actually Matters): While anyone can make a similar game, a public document makes it easy for a larger, well-funded studio to see your brilliant ideas, assign a team to execute them faster than you can, and release a similar game before you. This is the real risk you avoid by staying private.

You Can Change Your Mind Later: You can always switch a repo from Private to Public later when you're ready to share (e.g., when you have a playable demo). You can never switch a Public repo back to Private and undo the fact that the world saw your early, unpolished ideas.

The Case for Making It PUBLIC (The Risky Bet)
There are very few reasons to do this at your stage.

To Build Hype and a Community: If your goal was to be a "public figure" in game dev and build an audience around your design process, being transparent can be a tool. This is a valid strategy but requires constant engagement.

To Attract Collaborators: Making it public signals that you are open to others seeing your work and potentially joining the project.

Philosophical Belief in Open Source: If you genuinely believe all ideas should be free and open for others to build upon, then going public aligns with that philosophy.

For you, right now, reason #1 is not relevant, and reason #2 is premature. You are in the learning phase, not the collaboration phase.

The "What If" Scenario: What Happens if it's Public?
If your repository is public, remember the key distinction from earlier:

Protected: Your exact text (the specific lore you wrote, the exact wording of rules) is still copyrighted to you.

Not Protected: The ideas and mechanics within that text are now free for anyone to see and be "inspired" by.

A public repo doesn't mean you lose ownership; it just means you've shown your cards to the entire world.

The Verdict: What Should You Do?
Keep your GitHub repository PRIVATE.

You have already achieved the perfect setup:

Your Private GitHub repo is your legally timestamped vault for the full, detailed, secret design.

Your Public Itch.io page is your legally timestamped billboard that tells the world the core idea exists and that you own it, without giving the good stuff away.

This two-layer approach gives you the best of both worlds: ironclad legal proof and control over your intellectual property.

Do not switch it to public. The cons vastly outweigh the pros for someone in your position. The peace of mind that comes from knowing your unfinished, unpolished ideas are not being scrutinized is invaluable. Keep your vault locked while you learn to build.
